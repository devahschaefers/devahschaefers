Power is one of the numbers chip designers care about most, and one of the most
expensive to find out the hard way — you don't want to discover a design draws too
much only after it's fabricated. The question I worked on: can you predict a design's
power consumption directly from its structure, early, with machine learning?

## From netlists to graphs

A chip netlist is naturally a graph — cells connected by nets. The first half of the
project was a data problem: transforming raw netlist data into graph representations
that a model could actually learn from, across roughly 50–100 designs. I built the
pipeline with PyTorch Geometric to construct and featurize those netlist graphs,
including graph attention layers so the model could weigh which connections mattered.

## Modeling and benchmarking

Rather than betting on one approach, I trained and benchmarked a spread of them —
linear regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and a GNN — and compared them
on **MAE, RMSE, and MAPE**. I also benchmarked inference speed and scalability as
design complexity grew, since a model that's accurate but too slow to run across a
design space isn't useful in practice.

## What actually drives power

Accuracy alone doesn't build trust with hardware engineers — they want to know *why*.
I used SHAP analysis to surface the dominant features driving each power prediction,
turning the model from a black box into something that pointed at the structural
properties actually responsible for power draw.
